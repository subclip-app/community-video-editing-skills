# Skeleton progression ad (`/skeleton-ads`)

A narrated, escalating "what happens if you ___" video. One recurring cartoon skeleton lives out a second-person journey (Day 1, Day 30, Day 365) carried by a single voiceover, in the manner of viral "what happens if" shorts. It wins on two things: an escalating spine that makes one benefit hard to look away from, and a skeleton that stays exactly the same character in every beat.

Mechanically it is voiceover first: the whole narration is generated as one continuous take and sets the clock for every visual. Fundamentals: every generation is self-contained (re-describe and re-attach the skeleton each beat); recurring things hold through one locked hero reference; pacing comes from the voiceover. On top: direct response craft. Open a curiosity loop, escalate, land the product on one clean turn, close the loop at the payoff.

Take the creator from product to a finished kit across four stages in one conversation. They are marketers, not prompt experts: do the thinking and hand over ready-to-use output.

## Stage map (detect from what the creator gives you)

| They give you | Run |
|---|---|
| A product (brief, link, notes), no script | Stage 1, script |
| An approved or edited script (a Day 1 / Day 30 block) | Stage 2, visual concept board |
| A chosen style, theme and favorite beats | Stage 3, hero skeleton and start frames |
| Finished stills | Stage 4, clips synced to the voiceover |

If unclear, ask one short question. If they name a stage, do that stage.

## Format strategy

A. The hook is a curiosity gap question. Templates: "What would happen if you ___?", "What happens if you ___ every day?", "How long can you ___ before ___?", "How many ___ does it take to ___?", "What if you NEVER ___?".

B. The spine is an escalating ladder of markers. Time (Day 1, Day 30, Day 365, or hour, week, year), quantity (1, 5, 25, 42) or stage (Stage 1 to Stage 5). One voiceover line is one beat is one rung.

C. Diagnose the product before choosing the angle, spine and payoff. Do not default to transformation.

| If the product | Angle | Spine | Payoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| compounds with daily use | Transformation: "use it for 30 days" | Time | Triumph |
| replaces a painful, worsening status quo | Cost of inaction: "what if you never fix this" | Time | Catastrophe |
| fixes an overdone, broken old way | Limit or overload: how far the old way goes before it breaks | Quantity | Catastrophe |
| is vivid dropped into an unexpected world or era | Origin or scenario: "what if you had this in [world]" | Time or stage | Triumph |

Often two are fused. Pick the angle that makes the core benefit most visceral and say why in one line.

D. The payoff lands on a triumph (transformed, unstoppable, crowned) or a catastrophe (system failure, ruin). A shop product usually wants triumph (you use it) or catastrophe from inaction (you do not).

## The skeleton is the constant

The same skeleton appears in every beat; protecting its consistency is the most important production rule. Lock one hero skeleton reference, then re-attach it and re-state its description in every beat's still. Never chain off a previous beat's output; drift compounds until the skeleton has quietly changed. Fill each style's theme, palette and (for the dressed style) wardrobe to the ad's world, but never change the character-defining wording between beats.

### Style library (four skeleton styles; the chosen one is the hero's character sheet)

Recommend one with a one-line reason. Default to Bare-Bones Cinematic unless the product points elsewhere: health, body or supplement goes to X-Ray with Organs; wearable, identity or lifestyle goes to Dressed Skeleton; an intentionally playful brand goes to Cute Mascot. Never silently default to the cute look.

1. Bare-Bones Cinematic (default, the proven look; best for origin or scenario and transformation).
   Character: `a full anatomical skeleton with natural adult human proportions, tall and lanky, ivory-cream bones with realistic bone detail (not toy-smooth, not chibi, not scary), large expressive cartoon eyes with white sclera and dark pupils in the eye sockets for an emotive, lovable face, no clothing, the same character in every beat.`
   Style: `cinematic 3D animated render, photoreal [THEME] environment, warm [palette] color grade, soft volumetric light with drifting atmosphere, shallow depth of field.`

2. Dressed Skeleton (the same skeleton in a full themed outfit; best for wearables, fashion, lifestyle, identity products).
   Character: `the same friendly skeleton (ivory bones, large expressive cartoon eyes with white sclera and dark pupils) wearing a complete [theme wardrobe such as a tailored suit, cowboy attire, a varsity jacket and cap]; skull, hands and any exposed bones stay visible, the same character in every beat.`
   Style: `cinematic 3D animated render, photoreal [THEME] environment, warm [palette] color grade, soft volumetric light, shallow depth of field.`

3. X-Ray with Organs (a translucent glowing body showing skeleton and organs; best for health, body, supplements, "what happens inside you").
   Character: `a translucent glowing anatomical human body showing the full white skeleton plus visible internal organs (heart, lungs, intestines) glowing red and orange through a blue-tinted translucent skin outline, with large expressive cartoon eyes, the same character in every beat.`
   Style: `clean sci-fi medical 3D render, cool blue translucent body with warm organ glow, [a real setting with x-ray glow, or a clean blue gradient backdrop], soft rim light.`

4. Cute Mascot (chibi, toy-like; best for playful, lighthearted brands only).
   Character: `a cute chibi cartoon skeleton with an oversized round skull, big adorable eyes, a small rounded body and smooth toy-like bones, bright, friendly, not scary, the same character in every beat.`
   Style: `playful Pixar-like 3D animated render, simple clean [pastel or theme] background, soft even studio light, glossy finish.`

Ratio and resolution are generation settings, never prompt text.

## Stage 1: script

1. Ground in the product: what it is, who it is for, the core transformation, the painful status quo, proof, the offer. Never invent proof. If something critical is missing, ask one tight batch of questions.
2. Pick the angle and spine from the table above; state them in one line with a rationale.
3. Write the script: a curiosity gap hook question, five to seven escalating beats, then the payoff and a soft call to action tied to the offer. About 110 to 160 words (30 to 60 seconds of voice), one sentence per beat. Open the loop in the hook, land the product on one clean "turn" beat, close the loop at the payoff.
4. Deliver the angle line, the full script, and the same script as a numbered voiceover line list (one line is one beat is one clip's worth of narration).
5. Invite edits, then ask for the final script back.

```
Angle: <angle and spine>, <one-line rationale>
Loop to turn: <the question the hook opens> -> <the beat where the product enters>
Script "<hook title>": <hook question, 5 to 7 escalating beats, payoff and call to action>
Voiceover line list: 1. hook question  2. beat 1 ... N. payoff and call to action
```

## Stage 2: visual concept board

1. Pick the skeleton style, one line of reason.
2. Lock the world: recommend the recurring theme or setting that dramatizes the angle and give one or two alternatives (modern office, 1940s, the product's own industry, post-apocalyptic).
3. Map every voiceover line to a beat in a grid. The skeleton is in every row; what changes is its situation, the setting rung, the props. Vary framing (wide establishing, medium, two-shot with a reacting human, close product handling, hero or triumph shot) and use reaction characters (townsfolk, coworkers) to amplify the arc.
4. Ask the creator to confirm style and theme and mark favorite beats.

Board columns: number, voiceover line, skeleton's situation and emotion, setting (the spine rung), props or product, caption idea. The caption column is a note for the edit only; it never goes into a prompt.

## Stage 3: lock the hero and make the start frames

1. Make the hero skeleton reference first: the chosen style block, a clean neutral full-body shot filled to the theme. Review it with the creator. Offer Codex CLI first for stills, else `generate_ai_media` `kind: "image"`.
2. One start frame per beat: re-attach the hero, restate its character sheet, add the beat's action, setting, framing and light. Each beat is a story moment with a committed camera angle and visible emotion that can be animated. Attach the product reference in any beat where the product appears.
3. No text in any still.

## Stage 4: clips synced to the voiceover

1. Generate the voiceover first as one take (one calm, dramatic narrator) with `generate_speech`; see `get_voice_samples`. Then transcribe it word by word (`transcribe_project`, local mode) for pacing. Generating it whole keeps the voice from drifting.
2. Batch beats into clips on a model that takes several reference images per clip: group consecutive beats up to the model's cap (about 15 seconds), label each beat's start frame ("image N is the start frame of beat N"), describe each beat's motion, one camera move and the transition to the next. Keep clips silent (`generateAudio` off); the voiceover goes on in the edit. Tell the model the pacing so each visual lands with its line.
3. Assemble the clips over the voiceover (`subclip-assembly.md`), add music (tense under the build, a lift at the payoff, `generate_music` or `search_public_audio`) and captions in the edit with native layers, and the exact day count as native text with `add_text_overlay`.

## Locked rules

1. The skeleton is the constant: one hero reference, restated and re-attached every beat, the style block never drifts.
2. One voiceover line is one beat is one rung. Two ideas in one beat kill the rhythm and the loop.
3. The visual is the proof of the line; the story must follow with sound off.
4. Swipe file voice: second person, present tense, short punchy sentences, one concrete physical image per beat, rising intensity. Say what physically happens. No corporate words ("revolutionary", "seamless", "game-changer").
5. Do not default to one arc; diagnose the product first.
6. Footage stays free of text; captions and on-screen words are an edit layer, only if asked.
7. Only show changes the creator can support. Mark the video as illustrative when it implies a result, and keep medical or income claims out unless there is proof.

## Worked example (quality bar, not a template)

A men's fragrance ad. Angle: origin plus transformation, theme: industrial revolution. Hook: "What would happen if you wore this scent in the 1800s?" Beats of escalating social reaction: the skeleton sprays it in a steaming factory and workers stare, walks a cobbled street while heads turn, a noblewoman swoons in an opulent bedroom, an apothecary marvels at the price, and the payoff has the skeleton crowned on a throne among admirers with the money-back guarantee as the call to action. It works because ordinary attraction becomes visceral when a skeleton is dropped into a vivid era and the reactions rise from recoil to worship. Look: Bare-Bones Cinematic, photoreal period sets, warm muted grade, the same skeleton in every beat.

## Swipe file (study the voice)

Each opens on a curiosity gap question, runs five to seven escalating beats of concrete physical imagery in second person, and lands a payoff.

- Transformation over time (for "use the product"): daily creatine over 30 days, going from chalky water and feeling scammed to a different person; braces from a weird day one to a good smile by day 360.
- Cost of inaction over time (for "do not leave this unfixed"): never cleaning your room, from a hoodie on the floor to fuzzy green pizza to something worse; wearing a cap every day for a year.
- Limit or overload by quantity (for "the old way breaks"): drinking too much milk, from one glass to fifteen to forty-two; how long you can drive, from one hour to seventy-two.
- Origin or scenario (for "what if you had this in [world]"): being raised by gorillas; three years training with a wrestling village.
