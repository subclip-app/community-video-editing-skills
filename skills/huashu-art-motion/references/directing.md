# Directing a living picture

Adapted from the upstream breakdown, mechanism, frame-first, camera, palette and storyboard references. Numerical examples describe prior work, not Subclip measurements.

## Break down a reference

Read media context and existing contact sheets first. Record duration, aspect ratio and known frame rate from the project. Inspect targeted moments around a doubtful cut before asking for wider context.

For each shot record start/end, physical subject, composition, foreground/background, movement, transition origin, words and audio cue. Separate observations from hypotheses. Inspect a clean frame just before a transition; do not mistake transition debris for scene content.

Look for what moves inside the frame: limbs, cloth, smoke, waves, patterned borders and changing highlights. A wide camera move can hide that nothing in the painting moves. Compare moments after any entrance zoom settles. Do not call noise, film grain or global flicker meaningful object motion.

Write transitions as mechanisms: "eight vertical strips fall and rebound" or "tiles flip outward from the boat." "A fancy wipe" cannot guide implementation. Measure approximate duration from observed frames and state uncertainty. Automatic frame-difference heatmaps, onset detection and fitted BPM are not exposed as a dedicated tool here. Use targeted visual review and creator-supplied beat markers instead of claiming numerical analysis.

## Shot table

Put the following in the director brief, with one row per event:

| Time | Physical subject | Action | Camera | Screen words | Route |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 to 3 s | Parcel and gloved hands | Slide parcel onto a scale | Hold | None | Native media plus diagram |
| 3 to 6 s | Scale mechanism | Spring compresses and needle settles | Move to detail, then hold | Exact weight | HyperFrames |
| 6 to 9 s | Same parcel | Sender removes it and shuts the bag | Pull out | Optional label | Native media |

The example shows structure, not a reusable composition. Opening on a concrete action gives the viewer something to follow. End on the subject completing an action rather than a generic quote card.

Before production, check that each row names something visible and a verb. Keep evidence numbers identical in narration and on screen. Change composition between shots while retaining the chosen art direction. Do not make every shot a header with bullet points.

## Camera is punctuation

Ask where the viewer should look, whether it is readable already, whether the move is a transition or an accent, and what the previous camera action was.

| Move | Purpose | Starting point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hold | Reading, object motion, explanation, pause after a joke | As long as needed |
| Slow push | Reveal a detail not readable in the wide view | 0.6 to 1.5 s |
| Pull out | Explain location, finish a section, reveal the whole | 0.8 to 2 s |
| Glide | Follow a relationship or move between objects | 0.5 to 0.9 s |
| Fast push | A reveal, reversal or important number | 0.25 to 0.35 s, then hold |
| Whip | Sudden attention change or comic beat | About 0.26 to 0.30 s |
| Slam | One emphasis within a shot | About 0.17 s with a small settle |
| Hard cut | New shot size or reaction | Instant |
| Gentle drift | A long atmospheric painting | About 1 to 4 percent per second, judged in playback |

These are starting points, not quotas. In explainers, repeated slow pushes feel as mechanical as repeated fast pushes. Avoid three inward moves in succession. After a push, hold, pull out, glide or cut to another scale. Fast accents should be rare outside kinetic type or musical montage. A moving chart or real video often needs no extra camera motion.

Full-frame images tempt a "cut and push" rhythm. Show a detail by a pull-out, start a horizontal move from the previous subject, or arrange evidence on a shared desk. At a settled evidence close-up, the item should fill most of the view; a tiny photo on a huge beige desk defeats the purpose. Recompose landscape material for portrait rather than leaving empty bands.

## Frame-first design

For a new direction, design three compositions of the same story moment. Choose one before extending the sequence. Retain an already approved direction. If candidates require generation, include them in the paid quote.

For art research, inspect several distinct works or official scenes, usually at least three for a new range. Record visible traits, nearest-neighbor differences, medium and exceptions. A list of links is not proof that the works were seen. Do not borrow a famous painting's people and coordinates by default. A studio name needs a film or period scope, not an average filter across all its work.

Separate four things: visual language, composition tendencies, motifs, and action. Style can survive a new subject. Quiet painting does not require slow action. Describe color relationships and light rather than treating hex values as an artist's fixed palette.

## Palette

Use one main lightness range and 3 to 6 main colors, with derived shadows and mixed brush colors. Pick one accent for the current focus. Match real evidence or the selected art tradition. Avoid default blue/purple gradients, neon cyan/purple and purple glass unless requested or justified by the art direction. This is an upstream taste rule, not a reason to remove a requested historical blue painting.

## Six common failures

1. Slide rhythm: repeated headers, bullets and the same move. Replace each with one visible event and vary camera punctuation.
2. Copied demo: source characters, props and layouts enter the new story. Borrow techniques and redesign the content.
3. No concrete subject: abstract nouns become icons and words. Find the physical example before building.
4. Dead background or empty middle: a tiny animated island in a large blank frame. Enlarge the event and make background changes relevant. Intentional ink-painting negative space is different from a missing subject.
5. Redrawn evidence: existing photos or product output are replaced by invented diagrams. Keep real evidence and add explanation.
6. Inconsistent art: generic characters, unnecessary faces on objects and a new palette every shot. Match medium, edge, light and shape across people, props and world.
